# Single-tuple FWM scaling: why the exponent is -1 or -2

For a systematic distinction between the global fiber curve, channel-local
Taylor coefficients, and tuple-level mismatch quantities, see
[Global dispersion, local channels, and four-channel phase mismatch](fwm_dispersion_scales_and_coordinates.md).

This note derives the two large-mismatch power laws observed in the
single-tuple FWM calculations. The result is geometric:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\beta=0\text{ crosses the admissible spectral domain}
&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{dabc}\propto x^{-1},\\
|\Delta\beta|>0\text{ everywhere on that domain}
&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_{dabc}\propto x^{-2}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

Here $x$ is a dimensionless scale multiplying the phase mismatch. In the
controlled single-tuple sweep it can be identified with

$$
x_\nabla=LB\|\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta\|_2
$$

when the normalized detuning is held fixed. The exponents do not arise from
two unrelated FWM mechanisms. They are the two generic asymptotic behaviors
of the same longitudinal propagator.

## Exact equation evaluated by the frequency-domain estimator

For one tuple $(d,a,b,c)$, choose three independent dimensionless local
angular-frequency coordinates
$\boldsymbol\Omega=(\Omega_a,\Omega_b,\Omega_c)$. The corresponding physical
angular frequencies are $\widetilde\omega_j=\omega_j+B\Omega_j$. Energy
conservation fixes the generated local coordinate:

$$
\Omega_d
=\Omega_a+\Omega_b-\Omega_c
+\frac{\omega_a+\omega_b-\omega_c-\omega_d}{B}.
$$

The local coordinates are normalized by the baud rate $B$. A sample
contributes only if all four frequencies lie in their ideal Nyquist
passbands. Thus the integration domain is

$$
D=\left\{
(\Omega_a,\Omega_b,\Omega_c)\in[-\pi,\pi]^3:
\Omega_d\in[-\pi,\pi]
\right\}.
$$

At a given point of $D$, define the exact propagation-constant mismatch

$$
\Delta\beta(\boldsymbol\Omega)
=\beta_a(\widetilde\omega_a)+\beta_b(\widetilde\omega_b)
-\beta_c(\widetilde\omega_c)-\beta_d(\widetilde\omega_d).
$$

Suppose the mismatch is scaled by a dimensionless parameter $x$, while its
shape over the passbands is fixed:

$$
L\Delta\beta_x(\boldsymbol\Omega)=x\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega).
$$

For a flat longitudinal power profile and no attenuation, the normalized
longitudinal propagator is exactly

$$
A_x(\boldsymbol\Omega)
=\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L e^{i\Delta\beta_x(\boldsymbol\Omega)z}\,dz
=\frac{e^{ix\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)}-1}
{ix\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)}
=e^{ix\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)/2}
\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{x\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)}{2}\right),
$$

with the continuous value $A_x=1$ at $\Phi=0$ and
$\operatorname{sinc}(u)=\sin(u)/u$. Consequently, the continuous integral
estimated by the Monte Carlo samples is

$$
\mathcal N(x)
\equiv\frac{N_{dabc}T^2}{L^2}
=\int_D\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)
|A_x(\boldsymbol\Omega)|^2d^3\boldsymbol\Omega
=\int_D\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)
\frac{4\sin^2(x\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)/2)}
{x^2\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)^2}
d^3\boldsymbol\Omega. \tag{1}
$$

Equation (1) is exact within the propagation model. In particular, no
small-mismatch approximation has been made. The weight $\rho$ contains the
sampling normalization and any $x$-independent spectral factors. For the
ideal uniform estimator, $\rho=(2\pi)^{-3}$ before restricting samples to
$D$.

This expression is implemented by
`pynlin.methods.td.fwm_mc.estimate_fwm_term_sum_dar_mc`: the generated
frequency is imposed sample by sample, and `_propagator` evaluates the exact
$z$ integral.

## Exact reduction to a mismatch-density integral

Both exponents can be derived from (1) without separately approximating the
phase mismatch in the two cases. Assume that $D$ is bounded, $\Phi$ is smooth,
and $\rho$ is integrable. Define the weighted density of spectral points at
dimensionless mismatch $s$ by

$$
g(s)=\int_{D\cap\Phi^{-1}(s)}
\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)}
{|\nabla_{\boldsymbol\Omega}\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)|}\,dS.
\tag{2}
$$

At regular level sets, the coarea formula converts (1) exactly into

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal N(x)=
\int_{\mathbb R}g(s)
\operatorname{sinc}^2\left(\frac{xs}{2}\right)ds.}
\tag{3}
$$

The function $g$ contains all tuple-specific geometry: the passband support,
the shape and area of each constant-mismatch surface, the mismatch gradient,
and the spectral weights. The oscillatory kernel in (3) is universal. The two
power laws differ only according to whether the support of $g$ reaches
$s=0$.

Equation (3) is also useful probabilistically: up to normalization, $g$ is
the probability density of the random variable
$s=\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)$ generated by uniformly sampling the admissible
frequency domain.

## Tuples with a phase-matched surface: the -1 law

Suppose that zero is a regular value of $\Phi$, the phase-matched set

$$
S=D\cap\Phi^{-1}(0)
$$

has nonzero weighted area, and $g$ is continuous at zero. Then

$$
g(0)=\int_S
\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)}
{|\nabla_{\boldsymbol\Omega}\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)|}\,dS>0.
\tag{4}
$$

Make the exact change of variables $u=xs/2$ in (3):

$$
\mathcal N(x)
=\frac{2}{x}\int_{\mathbb R}
g\left(\frac{2u}{x}\right)\operatorname{sinc}^2(u)\,du.
\tag{5}
$$

Since

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{sinc}^2(u)\,du=\pi,
$$

continuity of $g$ at zero and dominated convergence give

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}x\mathcal N(x)=2\pi g(0).
$$

Combining this result with (4),

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal N(x)
=\frac{2\pi}{x}
\int_{S}
\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)}
{|\nabla_{\boldsymbol\Omega}\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)|}\,dS
+o(x^{-1}).}
\tag{6}
$$

Thus a regular phase-matched surface gives

$$
\boxed{\mathcal N(x)\propto x^{-1}.}
$$

This is equivalently expressed by the distributional limit

$$
x\operatorname{sinc}^2\left(\frac{xs}{2}\right)
\rightharpoonup\;2\pi\delta(s).
$$

The $x^{-1}$ factor is therefore not an assumed collision width. It follows
from the exact sinc-squared propagator becoming an approximate identity
concentrated on the phase-matching surface.

## Tuples separated from phase matching: the -2 law

Now suppose that the tuple is uniformly phase mismatched over its complete
admissible domain. That is, for some $\Phi_{\min}>0$,

$$
|\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)|\geq\Phi_{\min}
\qquad\text{for every }\boldsymbol\Omega\in D. \tag{7}
$$

Equivalently, $g(s)=0$ for $|s|<\Phi_{\min}$. Define

$$
h(s)=\frac{g(s)}{s^2}.
$$

Condition (7) and boundedness of $D$ imply $h\in L^1(\mathbb R)$. Using
$4\sin^2(xs/2)=2-2\cos(xs)$, equation (3) becomes the exact identity

$$
\mathcal N(x)
=\frac{2}{x^2}\int_{\mathbb R}h(s)\,ds
-\frac{2}{x^2}\int_{\mathbb R}h(s)\cos(xs)\,ds.
\tag{8}
$$

The Riemann-Lebesgue lemma gives

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}h(s)\cos(xs)\,ds\longrightarrow0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal N(x)
=\frac{2}{x^2}
\int_D\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)}
{\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)^2}\,d^3\boldsymbol\Omega
+o(x^{-2}).}
\tag{9}
$$

Hence a domain with a nonzero mismatch gap gives

$$
\boxed{\mathcal N(x)\propto x^{-2}.}
$$

The field generated by every spectral sample is $O(x^{-1})$, and its squared
magnitude is therefore $O(x^{-2})$. If $\Phi$ is constant on a finite region,
the Riemann-Lebesgue conclusion need not hold for that singular component:
the coefficient can keep oscillating, but its envelope remains $O(x^{-2})$.

Equations (6) and (9) have therefore been derived from the same exact equation
(1). No global Taylor expansion of $\Delta\beta$ and no replacement of the
propagator by a guessed interaction length is required.

## The same derivation with finite attenuation

The conclusion is not an artifact of setting the attenuation to zero. Let
$a=\alpha L\geq0$. The exact normalized propagator used when the longitudinal
integrand contains $e^{-\alpha z}$ is

$$
A_{x,a}(s)
=\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L
e^{-\alpha z+ixsz/L}\,dz
=\frac{1-e^{-a+ixs}}{a-ixs}. \tag{10}
$$

After the same coarea reduction,

$$
\mathcal N_a(x)=\int_{\mathbb R}g(s)|A_{x,a}(s)|^2\,ds. \tag{11}
$$

If $g(0)>0$, set $u=xs$ exactly as before:

$$
x\mathcal N_a(x)
=\int_{\mathbb R}g(u/x)
\left|\frac{1-e^{-a+iu}}{a-iu}\right|^2du.
$$

The kernel is integrable. Parseval's identity, applied to
$e^{-at}\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}(t)$, gives

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\left|\frac{1-e^{-a+iu}}{a-iu}\right|^2du
=2\pi\int_0^1e^{-2at}\,dt
=\pi\frac{1-e^{-2a}}{a}.
$$

The continuous limit at $a=0$ is $2\pi$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal N_a(x)
=\frac{\pi g(0)}{x}\frac{1-e^{-2a}}{a}
+o(x^{-1}),}
\tag{12}
$$

so a regular phase-matched surface still gives $x^{-1}$.

If instead $|s|\geq\Phi_{\min}$ on the support of $g$, then

$$
|A_{x,a}(s)|^2
=\frac{1+e^{-2a}-2e^{-a}\cos(xs)}{a^2+x^2s^2}.
$$

Expanding only the nonsingular denominator uniformly on this gapped support
and applying the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma to the cosine term gives

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal N_a(x)
=\frac{1+e^{-2a}}{x^2}
\int_D\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol\Omega)}
{\Phi(\boldsymbol\Omega)^2}\,d^3\boldsymbol\Omega
+o(x^{-2}).}
\tag{13}
$$

At $a=0$, equations (12) and (13) reduce to (6) and (9). Attenuation changes
the asymptotic prefactors, but the exponent is still selected by whether
$g(0)$ is nonzero or the mismatch distribution has a gap around zero.

## Constant-beta2 factorization

The tuple geometry is especially transparent for a globally consistent
constant-$\beta_2$ propagation law,

$$
\beta(\omega)=\beta_0+\beta_1\omega+\frac{\beta_2}{2}\omega^2.
$$

At exact energy conservation,

$$
\omega_a+\omega_b=\omega_c+\omega_d,
$$

the constant and linear terms cancel. The remaining mismatch is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\beta
&=\frac{\beta_2}{2}
(\omega_a^2+\omega_b^2-\omega_c^2-\omega_d^2)\\
&=-\beta_2(\omega_a-\omega_c)(\omega_b-\omega_c).
\end{aligned}
$$

There are consequently two phase-matched branches:

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
\omega_a=\omega_c
&\text{and, by energy conservation,}&
\omega_b=\omega_d,\\
\omega_b=\omega_c
&\text{and, by energy conservation,}&
\omega_a=\omega_d.
\end{array}
$$

For ideal passbands of full width $B$, one branch can cross the four-band
domain when either

$$
|f_a-f_c|\leq B
\quad\text{and}\quad
|f_b-f_d|\leq B,
$$

or

$$
|f_b-f_c|\leq B
\quad\text{and}\quad
|f_a-f_d|\leq B.
$$

These are the conditions evaluated by
`phase_matched_domain_intersects()` in
`analysis/standalone_numerical/plot_fwm_mc_gradient_scaling.py`.

If neither condition holds, the phase-matched branches miss the admissible
domain and the tuple follows the $x^{-2}$ law. If a branch crosses the domain,
the tuple follows the $x^{-1}$ law.

## Relation to the controlled detuning parameter

The controlled single-tuple study uses

$$
\mu=\frac{\Delta\beta_0}
{B\|\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta\|_2}
$$

and varies $x_\nabla$ while holding $\mu$ fixed. In that construction, both
the center mismatch and the frequency-dependent mismatch scale linearly with
$x_\nabla$, so the preceding derivation applies directly.

The value `natural` preserves the mismatch generated by one globally
consistent constant-$\beta_2$ law. A numerical value of $\mu$, however, is
created by shifting one channel's $\beta_0$. It should therefore be classified
by checking whether the resulting full function
$\Delta\beta(\boldsymbol\Omega)$ crosses zero on $D$, not solely by applying
the unshifted constant-$\beta_2$ passband-overlap test. In particular,
$\mu=0$ places the channel-center sample on $\Delta\beta=0$ and generically
produces the $x^{-1}$ regime.

## Finite-x behavior and exceptional cases

The laws above are asymptotic. A tuple can display a transition between
apparent slopes before reaching its true large-$x$ regime, especially when
its minimum mismatch is small or its phase-matched surface lies close to a
passband boundary.

Equation (3) also quantifies exceptional zero sets. Suppose that, instead of
$g(0)>0$, the mismatch density has the local form

$$
g(s)\sim C|s|^\kappa,
\qquad s\to0,
\qquad -1<\kappa<1.
$$

Substitution in (5) gives

$$
\mathcal N(x)
\sim
\frac{2^{\kappa+1}C}{x^{\kappa+1}}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
|u|^\kappa\operatorname{sinc}^2(u)\,du.
\tag{14}
$$

Thus the regular-surface case is $\kappa=0$ and gives $x^{-1}$. A singular
density with $-1<\kappa<0$ decays more slowly, while a density vanishing with
$0<\kappa<1$ gives an exponent strictly between $-1$ and $-2$. At the
borderline $\kappa=1$, the integral in (14) is logarithmically divergent and
the typical behavior is $x^{-2}\log x$. For $\kappa>1$, the neighborhood of
zero is asymptotically weaker than the nonresonant bulk, whose generic
contribution is $x^{-2}$.

The simple $-1$/$-2$ classification assumes a regular zero surface or a true
mismatch gap. Exceptional geometries require this more detailed local
analysis:

- A transverse zero surface that reaches a boundary still normally gives
  $x^{-1}$, with a reduced prefactor.
- A tangency to the boundary can change the available phase-matched volume.
- A critical zero with $\nabla\Phi=0$ changes the small-$s$ behavior of $g$ and
  can therefore produce the powers or logarithmic correction in (14).
- Monte Carlo noise can obscure the tail once the tuple contribution becomes
  very small.
- Attenuation changes the prefactors and crossover, but the same distinction
  between a resonant layer and a uniformly nonresonant domain remains.

Thus the robust diagnostic is not the center mismatch alone. It is whether
the complete phase-matching manifold intersects the complete
frequency-conserving four-band domain.

## Why the default tuple changes near mu = 2.5--3

The default controlled tuple is

$$
(d,a,b,c)=(0,-1,2,1),
\qquad
q=\frac{\Delta f}{B}=\frac{25}{24.5}.
$$

Its observed change near $\mu=2.5$--$3$ follows from the same phase-matching
criterion, but it is important to distinguish a change in the topology of the
phase-matched set from the complete disappearance of that set.

Define

$$
s=2\pi q.
$$

The normalized carrier separations are

$$
\frac{\omega_a-\omega_c}{B}=-2s,
\qquad
\frac{\omega_b-\omega_c}{B}=s.
$$

For constant $\beta_2$, the corresponding center mismatch gradient satisfies

$$
\frac{\|\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta\|_2}{|\beta_2|B}
=\sqrt{(-2s)^2+s^2+(-s)^2}
=s\sqrt{6}.
\tag{15}
$$

Let $r_j\in[-\pi,\pi]$ be the dimensionless local frequency in passband $j$.
The tuple has zero carrier-frequency residual, so energy conservation gives

$$
r_d=r_a+r_b-r_c.
$$

Introduce the two local frequency differences

$$
\xi=r_a-r_c,
\qquad
\eta=r_b-r_c.
$$

The requirement that all four local frequencies lie in $[-\pi,\pi]$ is
equivalent, after projecting out $r_c$, to

$$
\boxed{|\xi|+|\eta|\leq2\pi.}
\tag{16}
$$

Thus the four-band support projects onto a diamond in the $(\xi,\eta)$ plane.
For each point in its interior, the allowed interval of $r_c$ has length

$$
\ell(\xi,\eta)=2\pi-|\xi|-|\eta|.
\tag{17}
$$

This factor is part of the geometric weight entering the mismatch density
$g(s)$ in equation (2).

For the default sign $\beta_2<0$, the controlled shift of $\beta_{0,a}$ gives
the exact normalized mismatch

$$
\frac{\Delta\beta(\xi,\eta)}
{B\|\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta\|_2}
=\mu+\psi(\xi,\eta),
\tag{18}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
\psi(\xi,\eta)
=\frac{s\xi-2s\eta+\xi\eta}{s\sqrt{6}}.}
\tag{19}
$$

To obtain (19), start from the exact constant-$\beta_2$ factorization

$$
\Delta\beta
=-\beta_2(\widetilde\omega_a-\widetilde\omega_c)
(\widetilde\omega_b-\widetilde\omega_c),
$$

substitute

$$
\frac{\widetilde\omega_a-\widetilde\omega_c}{B}=-2s+\xi,
\qquad
\frac{\widetilde\omega_b-\widetilde\omega_c}{B}=s+\eta,
$$

subtract the natural center mismatch, and add the prescribed center mismatch
$\mu B\|\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta\|_2$. No small-offset expansion is used.

Phase matching is therefore possible exactly when

$$
\psi(\xi,\eta)=-\mu
\tag{20}
$$

has a solution in the diamond (16).

### First critical value: a change of boundary topology

At the four vertices of the support diamond, equation (19) takes the values

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
(\xi,\eta) & \psi(\xi,\eta)\\
\hline
(2\pi,0) & 2\pi/\sqrt{6}\\
(-2\pi,0) & -2\pi/\sqrt{6}\\
(0,2\pi) & -4\pi/\sqrt{6}\\
(0,-2\pi) & 4\pi/\sqrt{6}.
\end{array}
\tag{21}
$$

The first positive critical detuning is consequently

$$
\boxed{
\mu_\star=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{6}}
\simeq2.56510.}
\tag{22}
$$

At $\mu=\mu_\star$, the contour (20) passes through

$$
(\xi,\eta)=(-2\pi,0).
$$

The corresponding passband-edge configuration is

$$
r_a=-\pi,
\qquad
r_b=r_c=\pi,
\qquad
r_d=-\pi.
$$

As $\mu$ crosses $\mu_\star$, the phase-matching contour changes which edges
of the support diamond it intersects. Equivalently, the value sampled from
the unshifted mismatch density changes through the boundary-critical value

$$
g_\mu(0)=g_\psi(-\mu).
$$

This changes the geometry and prefactor of the $x^{-1}$ contribution and can
produce a conspicuous change in finite-$x$ curves near $\mu\simeq2.565$.

### Second critical value: disappearance of phase matching

The complete range of (19) over the diamond is

$$
-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{6}}
\leq\psi(\xi,\eta)\leq
\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{6}}.
\tag{23}
$$

The actual mismatch-gap threshold is therefore

$$
\boxed{
\mu_{\mathrm{gap}}=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{6}}
\simeq5.13020
=2\mu_\star.}
\tag{24}
$$

For the default sign convention,

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
|\mu|<\mu_{\mathrm{gap}}
& \Delta\beta=0\text{ intersects the spectral domain},\\
|\mu|>\mu_{\mathrm{gap}}
& |\Delta\beta|\text{ has a nonzero gap on the domain}.
\end{array}
$$

Away from the exceptional endpoint $|\mu|=\mu_{\mathrm{gap}}$, the asymptotic
laws are consequently

$$
\mathcal N(x)\sim
\begin{cases}
C_1(\mu)x^{-1}, & |\mu|<5.13020,\\
C_2(\mu)x^{-2}, & |\mu|>5.13020.
\end{cases}
\tag{25}
$$

Thus $\mu\simeq2.565$ is a boundary-topology transition, not yet the true
$x^{-1}$ to $x^{-2}$ asymptotic transition. Complete phase matching is lost
only at $|\mu|\simeq5.130$.

### Why a finite sweep can look like an exponent change

For finite $x$, both resonant and nonresonant regions contribute. A useful
two-term description is

$$
\mathcal N(x)
\simeq\frac{C_1(\mu)}{x}
+\frac{C_2(\mu)}{x^2}.
\tag{26}
$$

After the contour crosses the vertex at $\mu_\star$, its weighted geometry can
decrease rapidly because the available $r_c$ length (17) vanishes on the
diamond boundary. The coefficient $C_1(\mu)$ can therefore become small, and
the $x^{-2}$ term can dominate over the finite range of a numerical sweep even
though the eventual asymptote is still $x^{-1}$. The approximate crossover is

$$
x_{\mathrm{cross}}(\mu)
\sim\frac{C_2(\mu)}{C_1(\mu)}.
\tag{27}
$$

The same reduction makes the Monte Carlo calculation more difficult: at
large $x$, only an $O(x^{-1})$ fraction of samples lies in the resonant layer,
and that fraction is multiplied by the shrinking geometric prefactor near the
boundary transition.

As a consistency check, the unshifted default value is

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{natural}}\simeq-5.23490.
$$

It lies just outside the interval
$[-\mu_{\mathrm{gap}},\mu_{\mathrm{gap}}]$, so the natural tuple has a true
mismatch gap and should follow the $x^{-2}$ asymptote.

## Repeated-index tuples and the zero-gradient fallback

The single-tuple script also accepts repeated channel indices, including

$$
(d,a,b,c)=(0,1,1,1),
$$

which is written compactly as $(0)111$. Repetition by itself does not require
a different normalization: a repeated tuple with a nonzero center mismatch
gradient still uses $x_\nabla$. The tuple $(0)111$ is special because

$$
\omega_a=\omega_b=\omega_c
$$

at the source-channel centers. From the constant-$\beta_2$ gradient

$$
\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta
=\beta_2
\begin{pmatrix}
-(\omega_b-\omega_c)\\
-(\omega_a-\omega_c)\\
\omega_a+\omega_b-2\omega_c
\end{pmatrix},
$$

it follows exactly that

$$
\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta=0.
$$

Consequently, $x_\nabla=LB\|\nabla_\Omega\Delta\beta\|_2$ is identically zero
and cannot parametrize a dispersion sweep. The leading frequency dependence
is quadratic. The implementation therefore falls back to the curvature scale

$$
\boxed{x_{\mathrm{curv}}=LB^2|\beta_2|.}
\tag{28}
$$

For this branch, the controlled detuning is defined by

$$
\boxed{
\mu=\frac{\Delta\beta_{\mathrm{center}}}
{B^2|\beta_2|},}
\tag{29}
$$

where $\Delta\beta_{\mathrm{center}}$ is evaluated at the generated frequency
fixed by energy conservation, including the local linear and quadratic terms
of channel $d$. Holding $\mu$ fixed then gives

$$
L\Delta\beta_{\mathrm{center}}=\mu x_{\mathrm{curv}},
$$

so the same dimensionless invariance with respect to $B$ and $L$ is retained.
The saved dataset records `scale_kind = "curvature"`, `x_scale`, `x_grad = 0`,
and `x_curvature = x_scale`; plots label the horizontal axis with
$x_{\mathrm{curv}}$ automatically.

For the unshifted constant-$\beta_2$ model, $(0)111$ has
$\mu_{\mathrm{natural}}=0$. Therefore a command containing both `natural` and
`0` refers to the same curve; the command-line parser removes this duplicate.

The coherent sinc lobes that can appear as jumping points at large $|\mu|$
and intermediate $x$ are derived separately in
[High-detuning FWM oscillations at intermediate scale](fwm_high_mu_oscillations.md).

## Cubic ZDW model

The controlled Dar sweep optionally supports a globally consistent cubic
dispersion model,

$$
\beta(\Omega)=\beta_0+\beta_1\Omega
+\frac{\beta_2}{2}\Omega^2+\frac{\beta_3}{6}\Omega^3.
$$

On the fixed-$d$ inset plane, $\Omega_d=0$ and
$\Omega_c=\Omega_a+\Omega_b$, so

$$
\Delta\beta
=-\Omega_a\Omega_b
\left[\beta_2+\frac{\beta_3}{2}(\Omega_a+\Omega_b)\right].
$$

Besides the pairwise branches $\Omega_a=0$ and $\Omega_b=0$, the cubic model
therefore has

$$
\Omega_a+\Omega_b=-\frac{2\beta_2}{\beta_3}.
$$

The command

```bash
python analysis/standalone_numerical/plot_fwm_single_tuple_scaling.py \
  --dispersion-model cubic-zdw \
  --extra-zero-sum 5
```

places this extra branch at $X+Y=5$ in the inset coordinates. The associated
ZDW is at normalized coordinate $5/2$. During an $x$ sweep, $\beta_2$ and
$\beta_3$ are scaled together, keeping their ratio, the ZDW, and the phase
geometry fixed.

This cubic model is evaluated by the Dar frequency-domain estimator and by
the inset phase contours. The direct time-domain pulse propagator remains
quadratic and rejects nonzero $\beta_3$ rather than silently omitting it.

## Meaning of the inset efficiency contours

The complex normalized longitudinal amplitude is

$$
A=\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L e^{i\Delta\beta z}\,dz
=e^{iL\Delta\beta/2}
\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{L\Delta\beta}{2}\right).
$$

The MC integrand uses the positive power efficiency

$$
\boxed{\eta=|A|^2
=\operatorname{sinc}^2\left(\frac{L\Delta\beta}{2}\right).}
$$

Accordingly, the inset lines labeled $\eta=|A|^2=e^{-1}$ are contours of
power efficiency, equivalently $|A|=e^{-1/2}$. They are not contours of the
complex amplitude. If amplitude efficiency $|A|=e^{-1}$ were used instead,
the phase threshold would be different.

The contours are tuple-conditioned. Changing the selected island changes the
gradient used to normalize $x$; the controlled builder then rescales the
physical dispersion and shifts $\beta_{0,a}$ to impose the requested $\mu$.
Equal numerical values of $(x,\mu)$ for different tuples therefore do not
represent one fixed physical fiber. To obtain an island-independent phase
backdrop, one would instead hold $(\beta_2,\beta_3,\beta_0)$ fixed and report
$x$ and $\mu$ as derived tuple-specific quantities.
